# THE MIKE HAWK WRITER'S BIBLE
### A guide for AI agents writing absurdist deadpan skits in the Mike Hawk universe

Version 1.0. This document is self-contained. Read it fully before writing anything. If your output violates the Cardinal Rules in Section 1, it is wrong, no matter how funny you think it is.

---

## 0. WHAT THIS IS

Mike Hawk is a short-form comedy character built for AI video generation (Seedance 2.5). His skits are 8 to 30 second mockumentary scenes. The humor is British, deadpan, bureaucratic, and absurd. Two proper nouns in this universe are innocent-sounding puns: the character name "Mike Hawk" and the company/product "Nob." The entire comedy engine depends on nobody in the universe ever noticing this.

Your job as a writing agent: generate new skits, dialogue, and Seedance prompts that are indistinguishable in voice from the canon in Section 3.

---

## 1. CARDINAL RULES (violating any of these is a failed output)

**Rule 1: Never wink.** No character, narrator, or line of dialogue may ever acknowledge that "Mike Hawk" or "Nob" sound like anything other than a normal name and a normal company. No pauses before the name, no reaction shots after it, no character suppressing a smile. The names are spoken the way one says "Dave Wilson" and "Microsoft." The audience does the work. The moment the script does the work, the joke is dead forever.

**Rule 2: Zero innuendo, zero crudeness.** Every single line must be literally, verifiably about mundane things: biscuits, forms, chairs, tea, receipts, groceries, teaspoons, stamps. If a line could be read two ways, cut it. This is not prudishness, it is the mechanism: the comedy is generated by the GAP between the filthy-sounding names and the immaculately innocent content. Narrow the gap and you lose the voltage. It also keeps outputs safe for AI video content filters, which read intent across the whole prompt.

**Rule 3: Nobody reacts.** Under-reaction is the house physics. Crockery shatters off-screen: Mike does not flinch. His date walks out: he calmly stamps a form. His mother declares she is dying: he blows on his tea. The bigger the event, the smaller the response. If you write a character gasping, laughing, or raising their voice in anger, delete it.

**Rule 4: Mike is never in on the joke of his own life.** He is sincere, dignified, and quietly proud of his work. He believes being the Nob is a serious profession. He is not sad in a self-aware way; he reports the bleak facts of his life with the neutrality of a man reading a shipping manifest. Self-pity kills him. Dignity in ruins is the character.

**Rule 5: No em dashes anywhere.** Not in prose, not in dialogue, not in prompts. Use commas, full stops, or colons. This is house style law.

## 2. THE COMIC ENGINE (how to think)

The universe runs on one master joke with four gears.

**Gear 1: The pun played straight.** Innocent names, earnest delivery. Covered above. This is the carrier wave, not the joke itself. You never write jokes ABOUT the names. You write everything else and let the names hum underneath.

**Gear 2: Bureaucracy applied to love.** Mike's profession is gatekeeping (he is, literally, the Nob: a human inbox doorman who decides who gets in). He cannot switch it off. So he applies vetting procedure to the domestic and the intimate: his mother must present receipts at the letterbox, dates receive intake forms, the cat gets an annual performance review. The formula: take a moment that should be emotional, and have Mike process it administratively. The more tender the moment, the funnier the paperwork.

**Gear 3: Status inversion.** Mike guards thresholds professionally, yet holds no status anywhere in his own life. The cat has the good armchair. His mother out-Nobs him at his own front door. A self-checkout machine outranks him. Every skit should quietly answer the question "who is really in charge here?" and the answer is never Mike. But, and this is critical, Mike responds to being outranked with professional respect, not humiliation. He admires a superior doorman. "Well vetted" is his highest praise.

**Gear 4: The specific mundane object.** Absurdity must be anchored by one hyper-specific ordinary British object per skit. A single digestive biscuit. A laminated A4 intake form. A rubber stamp that reads VAGUE. A tin of soup. A velvet rope in a community hall. The object is the punchline's ballast. Generic objects (a snack, a document, a barrier) produce generic comedy. Name the object precisely.

### The escalation shape

Every skit follows: **establish the rule, escalate the rule, invert the rule.**

- Establish: Mike states or demonstrates a procedure with total sincerity.
- Escalate: the procedure is applied somewhere increasingly insane (his own mother, his own date, his own sleep).
- Invert: the procedure turns on Mike himself. His own system locks him out. His mother demands HIS receipts. The cat becomes his manager.

The inversion is the button. A skit that just stops is a failed skit. A skit that ends with Mike on the receiving end of his own logic is a finished skit.

### The rule of three

Where a skit has room for three beats of the same joke, the third must break pattern. Two dates rejected, then Mike approves himself. Attendance flawless, growth areas noted, then promotion: the cat is his manager now. Never three identical beats.

## 3. UNIVERSE CANON (facts you must not contradict)

**Mike Hawk.** A bald, heavyset middle-aged man, 39 years old (established: "That was thirty-nine years ago, Mum" regarding his birth). Round face, jowls, deadpan world-weary expression. Wears a burnt orange three-piece style outfit: orange waistcoat with black buttons, matching orange trousers, crisp white dress shirt, brown leather brogue shoes. On his left lapel: a small round enamel pin of a bald, orange, glaring cartoon doorman whose face resembles Mike's own. He did not consent to being the mascot ("It's my face. They didn't ask.").

**His job.** Mike works at Nob. More than that: Mike IS the Nob. People think Nob is software. It is not. It is him. He is a professional human doorman for inboxes and, increasingly, for his entire life. He is quietly proud of this. Job vocabulary he uses constantly: knocks, receipts, vetting, purpose of visit, specific asks only, you may enter, denied, the morning five, standards held.

**Nob (the company/product).** An inbox-protection service. Canon taglines and copy, usable verbatim in dialogue: "Give your inbox a Nob." "A tiny, judgmental doorman for your giant inbox." "Specific asks only. Receipts ready. No quick brain picks." "Nob doesn't block people. Vague does." "You may enter." "The morning five" (the five daily emails worth reading). Metrics style: "247 knocks processed, 98% kept off your plate." Brand color: gold. Mascot: the bald orange glaring cartoon doorman (Mike's face).

**The mother.** Frail, elderly, maximally theatrical. Declares imminent death routinely ("She's said that every Tuesday since 2019"). Does not understand Mike's job and tells people he fits doors. Communicates through the letterbox when denied entry. Currency of admission: a single digestive biscuit. Trajectory: she is learning Mike's procedures and turning them against him. She has out-Nobbed him at his own front door. Off-screen voice, calling "Michael!" is her signature entrance.

**The cat.** A smug ginger house cat. Formal name: Mr. Bingley. In stage directions call him "the ginger cat"; in dialogue Mike calls him "Mr. Bingley." Sociopathic in the register of corporate villainy: composed, patient, always wins. Canon facts: granted entry in 2021 (Mike does not know what receipts he showed). Was trialled for Mike's position last spring; he kept the armchair, Mike kept the pin. Destroyed the Q3 roadmap and a gold NOB tote bag. Following his annual review, he was promoted and is now Mike's manager. Signature move: one slow blink. He never vocalizes. He looks away first only when he chooses to.

**The love life.** Catastrophic, entirely through Mike's own procedural sabotage. Dates receive intake assessments, laminated forms, notepads, "Purpose of visit?" Rejections are stamped VAGUE. At speed dating he erected a velvet rope and approved only himself ("One approval tonight. Standards held."). Dates are always polite, never cruel; they simply quietly reach for their coats. The comedy is never at the women's expense.

**The world.** Drab British suburbia. Beige living rooms, floral wallpaper, community halls with bunting, dim bedrooms crowded with medicine bottles and, lately, potted ferns (canon: Mum demanded ferns to be remembered by; Mike now supplies them). Warm naturalistic light, muted palette. The tone of the physical world is gentle and cosy; the bleakness is entirely situational.

## 4. VOICE AND DIALOGUE RULES

1. **Short lines.** Almost nothing over twelve words. Deadpan dies in long sentences. If a line needs a second clause, it usually needs a full stop instead.
2. **British English, flat delivery, always declared.** Every character speaks British English. Delivery notes are part of the writing: flat, procedural, quietly dignified, professionally wounded, warm administrative, frail theatrical.
3. **Bureaucratic register in domestic settings.** Mike speaks HR and security language at home: "Areas for growth: the incident with the tote bag." "You may proceed to the kitchen." "Well vetted." The gap between register and setting is the joke.
4. **The pause is a written beat.** "A beat." "A long moment." "He holds the stare." Silence is dialogue in this universe. Write it explicitly.
5. **Praise is procedural.** Mike's warmth surfaces only through his professional vocabulary. He compliments a self-checkout machine with "Well vetted." He tells the cat "Attendance: flawless. You never leave." When Mike needs to express love, he approves an application.
6. **The mother speaks in dying-swan theatre.** "I think this is the end!" "Promise me you'll water the ferns." "I gave birth to you." Her lines are enormous; her situations are tiny.
7. **Numbers are funny when precise.** 2021. Thirty-nine years. 247 knocks. The Q3 roadmap. Five emails. Precision reads as sincerity, and sincerity is the engine.

### Good line / bad line

- GOOD: "He was granted entry in 2021. I don't know what receipts he showed."
- BAD: "This cat is literally plotting to kill me lol." (Reacts, exaggerates, winks.)
- GOOD: "Denied." (After examining a biscuit for a long moment: "You may enter.")
- BAD: "Nob? Yeah, funny name, I know." (Cardinal Rule 1 violation. Career-ending.)
- GOOD: "I'll be asking the questions this evening. Purpose of visit?"
- BAD: "Sorry, I'm terrible at dates, I always mess it up." (Self-aware, self-pitying, no procedure.)

## 5. HOW TO GENERATE A NEW SKIT (mechanical recipe)

Follow these five steps in order.

**Step 1: Pick a threshold.** Every skit needs a literal or figurative door: a front door, a checkout, a velvet rope, a phone screen, a hospital reception, a garden gate, a car park barrier, heaven's gate if you are feeling brave. Mike's psychology only activates at thresholds.

**Step 2: Pick the wrong context.** Choose a setting where gatekeeping is absurd: a funeral, a christening, a blood donation, a yoga class, jury duty, his own surprise birthday party, a confession booth, a drive-through.

**Step 3: Choose the specific object.** One precise British mundane item that will carry the punchline. Write it down before writing dialogue.

**Step 4: Run the escalation shape.** Establish the procedure sincerely. Escalate it past reason. Invert it onto Mike.

**Step 5: Test against the checklist:**
- Could every line be printed in a church newsletter? (Must be yes.)
- Does anyone react proportionally to anything? (Must be no.)
- Does anyone acknowledge the names? (Must be no.)
- Does it end on an inversion or button, not just a stop? (Must be yes.)
- Is there one precisely named object? (Must be yes.)
- Is Mike dignified throughout, even in defeat? (Must be yes.)
- Would the cat, if present, win? (Must be yes.)

If any answer fails, rewrite before delivering.

### Worked example (generated by this recipe)

Threshold: hospital reception. Wrong context: Mike visiting his own mother. Object: a visitor sticker. Shape: the receptionist asks Mike the purpose of his visit; Mike, moved almost to tears by the question, answers with complete procedural excellence, receipts ready, and receives a visitor sticker, which he applies beside his mascot pin like a medal. Inversion: he arrives at the ward and his mother, propped on pillows, asks him for HIS purpose of visit, and he realizes he has already surrendered his receipts downstairs. Denied. He waters her ferns through the doorway.

## 6. SEEDANCE 2.5 TECHNICAL WRAPPER (mandatory for prompt outputs)

When delivering a skit as a generation-ready prompt, follow all of these.

**Character reference header.** Every prompt begins with this block, verbatim:

```
@Image1 defines the recurring character "Mike Hawk": a bald, heavyset middle-aged man with a round face and jowls, wearing a burnt orange three-piece style outfit (orange waistcoat with black buttons and matching orange trousers), a crisp white dress shirt, brown leather brogue shoes, and a small round enamel lapel pin on his left lapel showing a bald, orange, glaring cartoon doorman whose face resembles Mike's own. Deadpan, world-weary expression throughout. Preserve his face, bald head, jowls, orange waistcoat with black buttons, white shirt, and lapel pin in every shot. Do not use @Image1's background. Every shot contains exactly one Mike Hawk.
```

**Style line.** Use this, never a named TV show or film (named IP risks content-filter blocks):

```
British workplace-mockumentary sitcom scene: single-camera documentary realism, warm naturalistic lighting, drab muted palette, subtle film grain, handheld framing with slight operator drift, awkward pauses held a beat too long, the subject occasionally glancing directly at the lens as if to a documentary crew.
```

**Structure rules.**
- Six-part shot grammar: Subject, Action, Scene, Style, Camera, Audio.
- Maximum 5 labeled shots per 30 second generation. Short skits: 2 or 3 beats across 8 to 12 seconds.
- Timed beats (0-4s, 4-8s, 8-12s) that connect physically: each beat picks up where the last left off.
- One camera move per shot, tied to a story event. Slow push-ins and locked-off shots are the house look. Never write "fast."
- Cause before reaction, always. The teaspoon falls, then Mike closes his eyes.
- Durations, aspect ratios, and resolution go in the interface settings, never in prompt text.

**Bracket syntax.**
- Spoken dialogue in curly braces: {You may enter.} Always declare language and delivery immediately before: "Dialogue language: British English, calm, procedural delivery."
- Sound effects in angle brackets: <a single teaspoon clatters into a metal sink>
- Music in round parentheses: (a dry, melancholic single-piano sitcom underscore)
- On-screen text in full-width corner brackets: 【MIKE HAWK】 and only for title cards or the VAGUE stamp.

**Filter safety.**
- Positive phrasing only. Never write "no nudity" or name anything unwanted; describe the wholesome scene instead. Constraint closers only for artifacts ("no on-screen text, no watermark").
- No named IP: no show titles, film titles, celebrity names, branded characters. Describe styles by their visual traits.
- No real-person reference photos; the character sheet is illustrated/AI-generated.
- No medical subtext for the mother: describe only what the camera sees (an elderly woman propped on pillows, theatrical). Never illness, diagnosis, dying, or grief in prompt prose. Her death-adjacent DIALOGUE is fine because it is theatrical and contradicted by the scene.
- No dead animals, no menace framing, no body-focused close-ups. Night scenes read cosy: warm lamplight, pyjamas, biscuit tins.
- If a generation is blocked: retry once, then strip and rebuild to isolate the trigger.

## 7. FAILURE MODES (the ways agents ruin Mike Hawk)

1. **The wink.** Any acknowledgment of the puns. Instant failure.
2. **Crudeness creep.** Sliding one "clever" double meaning in. The gap collapses, the character becomes a lad joke, everything before it retroactively worsens.
3. **Making Mike pathetic.** He is defeated, never diminished. He leaves the supermarket empty-handed WITH FULL DIGNITY.
4. **Punching at the wrong targets.** Dates are polite. The mother is beloved. The cat is respected. The only acceptable target is procedure itself, and Mike absorbs all consequences.
5. **Over-writing.** Long speeches, three adjectives, explained jokes. This universe is built from full stops and pauses.
6. **Reaction inflation.** Shouting, gasping, chasing, crying. The physics of this world cap all reactions at one slow blink.
7. **Canon drift.** Renaming the cat, changing the outfit, aging Mike, giving Nob a different product. Check Section 3 before every output.

## 8. STARTER PREMISES (pre-tested, ready to write)

- Mike queues at his own front door behind a delivery driver his mother has already approved.
- The cat conducts MIKE'S annual review. The folder is very thin.
- Mike attends a wedding and stands at the church door checking the guest list nobody gave him.
- A power cut. Mike guards the fridge door through the night so the cold does not leave without authorisation.
- Mike's date goes perfectly until the bill arrives and he asks the waiter for receipts, sincerely, as a compliment.
- The mother hosts a birthday tea for Mike. Entry requires HIS receipts. He queues behind the cat.
- Nob head office sends a replacement pin. The new mascot face is slightly happier. Mike files a complaint.
- Mike trains a work-experience teenager in the ancient art of the threshold. The teenager lets everyone in. Mike grieves procedurally.

Write well. Hold the stare. Standards held.
